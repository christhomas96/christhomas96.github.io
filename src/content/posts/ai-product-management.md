not the hype version. the version i've lived for the last six months building product at a startup while using an AI as a thinking partner on roughly half my significant decisions.

the obvious wins: faster first drafts of specs, PRDs, and user stories. decent rubber duck for logic-checking. good enough at pulling apart a vague requirement until it becomes concrete. these are real. they save maybe five to eight hours a week. that's not nothing.

but the less obvious thing — and i think this is more important — is that it changes what *thinking* costs. when a thought is expensive to develop, you think fewer thoughts. you pattern-match more, because pattern-matching is cheap. you skip the exploratory detour because you can't afford it. an AI interlocutor that's available at 2am and has infinite patience makes the exploratory detour free. you take it. sometimes the detour was the point.

what it doesn't change: judgment. prioritisation. the political read on why a stakeholder is pushing a particular agenda. the instinct that a technically correct solution is going to be socially rejected by the team. these things still require a human who has been paying attention. for now.

the PMs who will struggle aren't the ones who refuse to use AI. they're the ones who outsource judgment to it and call the output strategy.
